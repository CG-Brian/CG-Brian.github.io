## The challenge

A field technician faces a piece of industrial equipment that has stopped doing what it should. The information they need is spread across service manuals, maintenance records, alarm histories, parts catalogues and notes from previous sessions. They have to work out which symptoms matter, which root causes are plausible, what to check first, what to do, what evidence supports it, and how to document the session for the next person.

The platform supported that work with two complementary systems:

- **A conversational assistant** that answers technical questions, retrieves internal documentation, interprets the available fault context and explains troubleshooting information with grounded responses.
- **Guided troubleshooting**, a structured decision-tree workflow that takes a technician from symptoms to candidate root causes, diagnostic steps and corrective actions, and records the session outcome.

Answering questions is the easy part. The hard part is making the whole system dependable: repeatable workflows, evidence you can trust, guidance a technician can follow on site, and AI components that stay maintainable after the demo.

<Pull>
How do you combine probabilistic AI reasoning with deterministic troubleshooting workflows without giving up reliability, traceability or maintainability?
</Pull>

The platform deliberately splits two kinds of computation. Natural-language interpretation, evidence synthesis, tool selection, explanation and content enrichment are probabilistic, and that's where an LLM is useful. Tree traversal, workflow state, graph assembly, score computation and structural validation are deterministic, and they stay that way. Most of the decisions below come from respecting that boundary.

## Dynamic FM Guidance

<StoryBrief
  status="Proposed & prototyped · presented to the customer · not a production feature"
  problem="Guided sessions walked every technician through the same predetermined yes/no tree for a fault code, even when they already knew the answers to several of its questions."
  contribution="I proposed a symptom-driven enhancement to the existing decision trees, explored it in a prototype, and presented the concept in a customer-facing discussion."
  outcome="A design that makes the validated tree do less unnecessary work instead of replacing it, with clear rules for when confirmed symptoms may narrow or only reweight the candidates."
/>

A technician often arrives with useful information already in hand: an alarm on the panel, an abnormal reading, a visible condition, the result of a quick check. The static workflow still asked about all of it, one step at a time. That's correct behavior and inefficient guidance.

My proposal was to let confirmed observations shape where the existing traversal starts and what it still needs to ask:

<Figure caption="Synthetic fault, observations, questions and weights. The proposed path is a prototyped design, not deployed runtime behavior, and no measured question reduction is implied.">
  <DynamicFMGuidance />
</Figure>

### Two ways to give the system context

- **A structured checklist.** Technicians select known symptoms from predefined options. It's deterministic, easy to validate, works in constrained environments, and maps directly to known symptom identifiers.
- **Free text.** Technicians describe what they see in their own words. An AI component extracts candidate symptoms and maps them to existing identifiers where possible, and the technician reviews and confirms the result. The system never silently treats an uncertain extraction as a fact.

### Design decisions

- **Keep the existing decision tree.** The validated guided-troubleshooting engine stays in charge of traversal. Symptom evidence influences where and how it proceeds; an LLM never improvises the path.
- **Map to existing symptoms.** Observations aren't new graph nodes. They have to resolve to known symptom identifiers and the tree's metadata before they can affect anything.
- **Confirm before filtering.** A candidate extraction is not evidence. Answers are explicitly yes, no or unknown, and only confirmed answers justify strong decisions. An unknown stays a question.
- **Skip what's already answered.** Questions covered by a confirmed observation aren't asked again.
- **Keep a fallback.** Technicians can always continue through the standard step-by-step tree when symptom information is incomplete or unreliable.

The central design question was what a confirmed symptom should be allowed to do to a possible cause:

### Prototype and exploration

Building the prototype meant working through the existing decision-tree structure, symptom metadata and cause-symptom relationships, the recursive splitting and traversal logic, and how known observations could change candidate selection. It also showed how the deterministic troubleshooting score (below) could carry the prioritization. The approach I explored used confirmed symptoms to shape the starting candidate distribution, marked symptoms that were already used as answered, and then continued through the existing recursive tree logic.

That's a prototype and a design, not a shipped feature, and I haven't measured a reduction in questions or diagnostic time.

<Pull>
Making a deterministic workflow smarter doesn't always mean replacing it with an LLM. Sometimes AI should interpret the starting context, and the validated workflow should simply do less unnecessary work.
</Pull>

## Engineering the main conversational agent

<StoryBrief
  problem="The main assistant had to balance domain relevance, technical accuracy, retrieved evidence, user intent, readability, workflow boundaries, language and guardrails, and do it predictably for every request."
  contribution="I substantially refined the assistant's prompting architecture into explicit, structured sections, and iterated on it against representative troubleshooting requests and retrieved context."
  outcome="A clearer behavioral contract: grounding rules that don't get lost among formatting instructions, intent-specific guidance that can't override mandatory constraints, and a firm boundary with the guided workflow."
/>

The goal wasn't to make the model sound more polished. Its answers had to meet behavioral requirements that other parts of the system, and the technicians, could rely on. I organized the prompt into XML-style sections, each with one responsibility:

<Figure caption="Prompt responsibilities, not separate model calls. Section names are illustrative; the actual prompts stay private.">
  <PromptArchitecture />
</Figure>

The decisions that mattered most:

- **Evidence policy stands on its own.** Grounding rules are stated separately from formatting, so the model uses retrieved evidence instead of inventing equipment details or unsupported procedures.
- **Mandatory requirements outrank intent guidance.** Some rules apply to every answer. Others depend on whether the user wants an explanation, troubleshooting advice or equipment information, and those contextual hints can't override the global contract.
- **Language follows the user.** Responses use the technician's language where supported, while keeping required technical identifiers and terminology intact.
- **The workflow owns the workflow.** The assistant can explain symptoms and provide information, but step transitions and guided-session state belong to the deterministic decision-tree system, and the prompt says so explicitly.
- **Answers are built for use.** Troubleshooting responses lead with actions, conditions, warnings and concise references, because a technically correct paragraph can still be unusable on site.

Prompt architecture doesn't work in isolation. Guardrails decide which requests reach it, retrieval decides which evidence it can use, and the response contract makes output consistent enough for the rest of the system to depend on. I didn't measure a single accuracy number for the main chat; improvements were judged against representative requests and their retrieved context.

<Pull>
Prompt engineering for an enterprise agent is behavioral interface design: a contract between user intent, retrieved evidence, model behavior and downstream software.
</Pull>

## Retrieval & grounding

### Debugging the evidence pipeline

<StoryBrief
  problem="Generated troubleshooting workflows sometimes omitted the replacement parts and tools a technician would need. It looked like missing documentation."
  contribution="I traced the retrieval path end to end, rebuilt query construction around components and the documentation's language, and reused evidence that earlier pipeline stages had already found."
  outcome="In one nine-cause validation example, causes without extracted parts fell from 5 to 3 and parts entries rose from 10 to 16."
/>

Rather than assume the index was incomplete, I traced what the pipeline actually did. I looked at the available component metadata, the generated query text, the chunks that came back, the language of the query versus the language of the documents, and whether relevant source evidence was being reused.

Two problems stood out. Queries were frequently assembled from long **German** descriptions, while the useful technical documentation was often in English or another language. And the queries emphasized cause-and-action narratives over the component actually being searched for, so the useful signal was diluted. In an initial sample of 27 search cases, the existing strategy found no relevant document in the top three for any of them.

<Figure caption="Synthetic query and documents. The counts at the bottom come from one nine-cause validation example: useful evidence, not a benchmark.">
  <RetrievalDebug />
</Figure>

I changed query construction to emphasize the relevant equipment components, technical identifiers and terms aligned with the target documentation, and to reuse evidence already retrieved upstream. I also refined the grounding behavior for parts and tools generation.

One trade-off is worth stating. My first relevance filter relied on token overlap and domain-specific stopwords. Review feedback rightly called it brittle, because I'd applied a deterministic rule to a semantic problem. The final version keeps deterministic normalization for exact identifiers and leaves contextual relevance to semantic grounding.

The nine-cause numbers are a small validation example, not a production-wide measurement, so I don't generalize them into a retrieval-accuracy figure.

<Pull>
Before changing the model, inspect the retrieval query and the evidence it returns. An LLM can't use evidence the system never retrieved.
</Pull>

### Image relevance filtering

<StoryBrief
  problem="Retrieval returns images from any broadly relevant document, so answers collected diagrams that had nothing to do with what was actually said."
  contribution="I implemented image relevance filtering, then moved it from right after retrieval to after answer generation, and made it fail-open."
  outcome="The filter now judges relevance against the answer that was actually written, and a filter failure never blocks the main troubleshooting response."
/>

My first version filtered images immediately after retrieval, which meant it was judging relevance to an answer that didn't exist yet. After generation, the filter can consider the question, the generated answer and the candidate images together.

<Figure caption="Same model call, different moment in the pipeline, different information available.">
  <ImageFilterPlacement />
</Figure>

Because images are a supporting feature, the filter fails open. If it errors, the technician still gets the answer, possibly with a few extra images. The filter runs in both the conversational and diagnostic-content workflows.

<Pull>
The right place in a pipeline for an AI judgment depends on what the system knows at that stage.
</Pull>

## Diagnostic algorithms & workflows

### Deterministic troubleshooting scoring

<StoryBrief
  problem="A diagnostic workflow can surface several possible root causes with no signal about which to check first, and an LLM-generated ranking would be hard to reproduce."
  contribution="I designed and implemented a deterministic troubleshooting score built on expert-provided signals, integrated it into diagnostic-tree generation, and unit-tested its mathematical behavior."
  outcome="Reproducible, explainable priorities with no extra model calls, and the prioritization signal the Dynamic FM Guidance prototype builds on."
/>

The score combines occurrence likelihood, testing effort and corrective-action effort. Occurrence is weighted most heavily, effort applies a penalty, and the results are normalized within the relevant fault context so causes can be compared directly.

<Figure caption="Illustrative causes and numbers, not customer data. The real formula and its coefficients stay private.">
  <PriorityDistribution />
</Figure>

The implementation details were where correctness lived:

- Defaults for missing expert inputs are applied **at calculation time**. The source data is never rewritten as though an expert had supplied the value.
- Edge cases are deterministic, and a single cause normalizes correctly.
- Normalization holds for any number of causes.
- Unit tests cover the boundary cases and the score's integration into the tree-generation pipeline.

### Tree construction and graph contracts

<StoryBrief
  problem="Generated diagnostic trees were structurally valid, yet the frontend and workflow service sometimes presented an alternative branch before the intended primary path."
  contribution="I traced the issue to how the tree builder inserted edges and introduced two-pass graph construction, applying the same fix in both builder paths where it appeared."
  outcome="Downstream consumers receive the primary path first, protected by explicit edge-order assertions and graph-level regression tests."
/>

The downstream services expected each step's outgoing edges in a particular order, but no schema said so. While building a chain, the builder attached some alternative edges immediately and added primary-path edges later, so the serialized order didn't match what consumers read.

<Figure caption="Generic Primary / Alternative labels. No real diagnostic content.">
  <GraphOrdering />
</Figure>

The two-pass version builds and connects the primary path first, collects alternative branches in a pending set, then attaches them once the primary structure is complete. Tests now assert outgoing edge order explicitly, and regression snapshots guard the overall graph.

<Pull>
Valid structured data can still break a downstream contract. When consumers rely on ordering, ordering is part of the interface.
</Pull>

### Additional AI capabilities

For the diagnostic authoring workflow, I implemented two further backend capabilities:

- **Structured diagnostic extraction** turns supplied free text into structured diagnostic information.
- **Diagnostic-path validation** compares a proposed diagnostic path against supplied reference paths.

Both use structured output contracts and bounded input context, and both are independently testable. They're deliberately self-contained: they work only from context the caller provides and make no additional outbound service calls, which keeps their scope narrow and their availability independent of other services. The trade-off is that their data is only as fresh as what the caller sends. I also kept a strict naming distinction between *newly extracted* information and *existing candidates* supplied for comparison, so suggestions are never confused with references.

## Evaluation & production reliability

### Guardrail evaluation

<StoryBrief
  problem="The assistant had to reject genuinely out-of-scope requests without blocking valid industrial support questions, but the guardrail was rejecting legitimate requests too, such as equipment-related network configuration questions."
  contribution="I tuned the guardrail prompts and configuration iteratively against a labeled evaluation set of 82 cases across 10 categories, checking false positives and false negatives in every category."
  outcome="Fewer legitimate requests refused, without loosening the categories that should stay blocked. No single accuracy figure is claimed."
/>

The categories spanned valid domain questions, casual off-topic chat, ambiguous boundary cases, adversarial requests and jailbreak attempts. Every evaluation run needs real LLM inference, with its cost, latency and nondeterminism, so these runs were deliberately kept separate from deterministic unit tests. A stricter guardrail isn't automatically a better one; the target is the right decision for each context.

### Testing and CI

<StoryBrief
  problem="The agent repository mixed deterministic unit tests, server-dependent tests, external-service integration tests and LLM-dependent evaluations, and automated validation didn't run consistently on feature and fix branches."
  contribution="I led a three-phase effort to classify tests by their real dependencies, run the right checks on every branch, and remove an unnecessary server dependency."
  outcome="Deterministic checks gate changes reliably, model-dependent evaluations run on their own terms, and a release-pipeline regression was caught before merge."
/>

1. **CI integration.** I extended validation to feature and fix branches, with Python checks and pytest results published in a machine-readable form.
2. **Test classification.** I classified the suite by actual dependency and introduced explicit integration-test markers, so deterministic tests run on every change. I wrote a technical briefing for backend and QA collaborators to agree the scope first.
3. **Self-contained tests.** I traced the app's dependency-injection setup and converted a server-dependent test into an **in-process ASGI test** with an in-memory database.

<Figure caption="Deterministic checks gate the merge; model-dependent evaluations run separately.">
  <CIBeforeAfter />
</Figure>

During the refactor I also noticed that a build step slated for removal had a second job: preparing an executable path that a later release step relied on. I made that dependency explicit before the change merged, so the release pipeline kept working.

At a recorded snapshot, the repository's deterministic suite stood at 269 passing tests. That's the team's suite, not tests I wrote alone; my contribution was making it run on the right branches, against the right boundaries.

## Lessons learned

Nearly every problem lived in a different layer, and each layer needed a different kind of fix.

<Figure caption="Related, but distinct, failure domains.">
  <FailureDomains />
</Figure>

### Improving a system can beat replacing it

Dynamic FM Guidance showed me that a deterministic workflow can become more efficient by using confirmed context, without handing execution control to an LLM.

### Prompts are behavioral contracts

A conversational agent depends on explicit grounding, scope, response structure, and a clear boundary between AI reasoning and deterministic workflows.

### Diagnose the correct failure layer

Retrieval failures, schema mismatches, graph ordering and prompt behavior each needed a different fix. Not every AI-system problem is solved by changing the prompt.

### Data and software contracts matter

Report generation and guided-session integration showed that wrong assumptions about structured data produce convincing but incorrect output.

### Reliability extends beyond model behavior

Testing strategy, integration boundaries, CI and failure handling are as much a part of delivering an AI feature as the model call itself.

<Pull>
Building reliable AI systems is less about adding an LLM to every problem and more about deciding where reasoning belongs, where deterministic logic should stay in control, and how the two can work together safely.
</Pull>
